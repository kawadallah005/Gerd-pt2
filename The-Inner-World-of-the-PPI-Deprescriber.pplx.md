## PART I: Identity & Core Beliefs

### 1. Who is your customer?

The PPI Deprescriber is 45-70 years old, split close to evenly by gender, and has been taking a daily proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole, esomeprazole/Nexium, lansoprazole, pantoprazole) for anywhere from 2 to 20+ years. They were very likely prescribed it for a short course that was never formally revisited.

- They see themselves as someone who follows medical advice responsibly, which is precisely why this situation frustrates them: they did what they were told, and yet nobody ever came back to check if they still needed it.
- A named, real example from this research: Rocky Angelucci initially considered Prilosec (omeprazole) **"a miracle drug"** for his acid reflux, but every time he tried to stop over the following decade, he felt worse than before he started (The People's Pharmacy, peoplespharmacy.com/articles/getting-off-omeprazole, accessed 2026-09-13). He eventually succeeded in quitting for good after roughly eight years of attempts.
- Many are managing 1-2 other chronic conditions simultaneously (polypharmacy is common in this population, per PubMed research on internal-medicine deprescribing populations, accessed 2026-09-13, previously catalogued in the Emerging Avatar Opportunity Report, Avatar #1), so their self-image includes "person who manages a lot of medications carefully," not someone who takes pills casually.
- They take pride in being their own health advocate once they start researching this, actively comparing tapering schedules and reading pharmacology forums rather than passively accepting "just keep taking it."

### 2. What attitudes and beliefs shape their worldview?

- A belief in personal responsibility paired with a growing sense that the medical system did not hold up its end of an implicit bargain: prescribe short-term, then review. One statistic captures this precisely: in a patient-awareness study, 87.8% of surveyed physicians were generally or well aware of PPI adverse effects, but only 14.6% actually explained them to patients (PMC, "Awareness of Proton Pump Inhibitor Adverse Events," accessed 2026-09-13). A separate study found 96% of a patient sample was unaware of PPI adverse effects at all (PMC, "Patient Awareness of Reported Adverse Effects Associated with Proton Pump Inhibitors in a Medically Underserved Community," accessed 2026-09-13).
- "Fairness" here means being told the full picture up front, not discovering years later, often through their own research, that a drug they trusted had risks nobody mentioned.
- A pragmatic, not conspiratorial, default stance: most in this population aren't fringe anti-pharma, they're disappointed insiders. But the door to a harder-edged "this is a business model, not a mistake" belief is open and gets walked through by some, especially once they learn how PPI rebound works (see Part IV).
- **Core life philosophy, in one to three sentences:** I trusted a professional recommendation and I'm not sorry I did, but I deserve to know what a medication is doing to my body over 10+ years, not just over the first 8 weeks. If something safer exists, I want the chance to try it, carefully, not recklessly.

### 3. What are their hopes, dreams, and goals?

- The dominant hope isn't "cure GERD forever," it's **"get to a place where I'm not dependent on this pill just to function."**
- An ideal outcome, drawn from a real reader account: after roughly a year of gradual tapering, one person now controls their reflux "very well by eating a very low-carb diet and taking DGL as well as famotidine when I do have symptoms" (People's Pharmacy reader comment, peoplespharmacy.com, accessed 2026-09-13, verbatim per search summary) — the goal is visibly not zero medication forever, it's *control without daily dependency*.
- A UK deprescribing study offers a genuinely hopeful, real data point worth internalizing for messaging: at 12 months, 75.1% of eligible patients had stepped down or off their PPI entirely (35.3% stepped off completely, 5.0% stepped down then off, 34.8% stepped down only) through a structured program of education plus alginate rescue therapy (PMC, "Sustained proton pump inhibitor deprescribing among dyspeptic patients in general practice," accessed 2026-09-13). This avatar's hope is statistically well-founded, which is a legitimate, honest thing to tell them.

### 4. What are their struggles, frustrations, and fears?

**Internal:**

- Fear that stopping will trigger something worse than the original problem. Rocky Angelucci's account describes what happens without a careful taper: **"horrible burning, choking reflux"** and a sensation like he had **"swallowed a potato and it was stuck halfway down, along with burning acidity"** (People's Pharmacy, accessed 2026-09-13, verbatim per search summary).
- A verbatim reader account captures the trapped feeling directly: **"I switched to omeprazole that I buy over the counter. When I read the label it said not to take it for more than 14 days in a row. As soon as I stopped, the pain in my stomach was unbearable. I have to keep taking it."** (People's Pharmacy reader comment, peoplespharmacy.com/articles/stopping-omeprazole-provoked-unbearable-pain, accessed 2026-09-13, verbatim per search summary).
- A Quora answer shows the specific dread of long-term risk compounding: a respondent describes trying to quit, getting acid reflux back "that could eventually cause cancer" when they miss a dose, alongside back and leg pain and "early degenerative changes in their spine," concluding "the long term use is really bad" (Quora, "I've been on omeprazole for over 15 years now...", accessed 2026-09-13, near-verbatim per search summary).
- On HealthUnlocked, a poster describes being "a bit scared" about weaning off omeprazole after "horrendous issues" from stopping cold turkey previously (healthunlocked.com/pmrgcauk/posts/152117985/stopping-omeprazole, accessed 2026-09-13, paraphrase per search summary).

**External:**

- Drugs.com's aggregated patient rating for omeprazole sits at 4.7 out of 10 across 513 reviews, with only 32% reporting a positive experience and 51% reporting a negative one (drugs.com/comments/omeprazole, accessed 2026-09-13) — a genuinely useful, honest statistic showing this is not a universally beloved drug even among people currently prescribed it.
- Reported side effects volunteered by patients cluster around fatigue, brain fog, anxiety, heart palpitations, joint and body aches, and tingling in the extremities (drugs.com/askapatient.com aggregate summary, accessed 2026-09-13).

**What they worry about most often:** that the drug that fixed their symptoms is quietly causing a different, slower problem (bone density, kidney function, nutrient depletion, even cancer risk in the more anxious framing found on Quora) that won't show up until it's serious.

### 5. What do they believe is holding them back?

- **A prescribing culture that treats "chronic" as a permanent, unquestioned label** rather than a status to be periodically re-evaluated. Many were prescribed PPIs for a short 6-8 week course and simply never had it revisited (per TikTok pharmacist-educator content summarized in this research, accessed 2026-09-13).
- **The rebound effect itself**, which this avatar increasingly understands as a specific physiological trap, not proof their disease is incurable: stopping a PPI causes the stomach to temporarily overproduce acid because it had compensated for suppressed acid production, and that overproduction is misread as "see, I still need this" (search-indexed clinical explainer content, accessed 2026-09-13).
- **A lack of an obvious, doctor-endorsed off-ramp.** Several tapering schedules exist informally in patient communities (a common one: 20mg for two days, then 10mg on the third, repeated for two weeks, then alternating 20mg/10mg for two more weeks before switching fully to 10mg, per HealthUnlocked-sourced patient-shared schedules, accessed 2026-09-13), but there is no single, widely known, doctor-given standard, which leaves patients improvising.

### 6. What are their prejudices or judgments?

- A judgment against being treated as a permanent customer rather than a patient whose treatment plan should evolve. This shows up sharply in the "lifelong customer" framing found in category commentary: the idea that "treating a chronic disease is profitable, but curing it is a financial disaster" (drhoffman.com, discussing pharmaceutical-industry criticism, accessed 2026-09-13) — whether or not this avatar fully endorses this as a deliberate conspiracy, the underlying resentment (why wasn't I told, why does this feel designed to keep me here) is real and worth taking seriously as a belief, not dismissing as fringe.
- A related, more fringe-adjacent belief surfaced in this research holds that heartburn is sometimes caused by *low* stomach acid (hypochlorhydria), not excess acid, and that PPIs are therefore treating the opposite of the real problem for some patients (adamrinde.substack.com, untreated.substack.com, accessed 2026-09-13). Mainstream sources are careful to note this remains debated and unproven for the general reflux population, and that chronic PPI use itself can eventually cause the acid-secreting glands to underperform (Cleveland Clinic and PMC sources, accessed 2026-09-13) — this belief should be treated in marketing as a "here's a debate you may have heard of" acknowledgment, never as an accepted fact to build a health claim on.
- Judgment toward "cold turkey" quitters as reckless, paired with real self-recognition that they were once tempted to do exactly that out of frustration.

## PART II: Market & Solution Perception

### 7. What existing solutions or products are they using?

- The PPI itself, still, in most cases, alongside active research into alternatives.
- **DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice)** is the single most frequently mentioned specific supplement alternative found in this research, both from patient reports and from category information sources (Healthline, Wikipedia's Deglycyrrhizinated licorice entry, accessed 2026-09-13). One supplement reviewer's comment was notably combative toward the pharmaceutical alternative: **"This works better than what the pharmaceutical companies make, without the massive side effects such as Osteoporosis and renal/kidney failure."** (search-indexed supplement review summary, accessed 2026-09-13, verbatim per search summary).
- **Famotidine (an H2 blocker)** used as a step-down bridge medication, exactly as in the People's Pharmacy reader's successful account above.
- **Alginate rescue therapy** (Gaviscon-type), specifically validated in the UK deprescribing study cited above as part of a successful structured program.
- **Persimmon tea** is mentioned as a folk/home remedy readers share with The People's Pharmacy specifically in the context of PPI discontinuation (peoplespharmacy.com, accessed 2026-09-13).
- **Low-carb dietary changes**, self-reported as a meaningful lever by more than one source in this research.

### 8. What does the market like about existing solutions?

- Successful tapering, when it works, is described with visible relief and a sense of regained self-management, not triumph: **"I now control my reflux very well by eating a very low-carb diet and taking DGL as well as famotidine when I do have symptoms"** (People's Pharmacy reader, accessed 2026-09-13, verbatim per search summary).
- On HealthUnlocked, one successful taper is described plainly: tapered "very carefully over 8 weeks and has been off for 6 weeks with no problems and no acid reflux" (healthunlocked.com, accessed 2026-09-13, paraphrase per search summary).
- Structured, education-plus-alginate programs show strong satisfaction in the aggregate data (75.1% stepped down or off at 12 months, cited above), suggesting the market genuinely responds well when given a credible, structured off-ramp rather than being left to guess.

### 9. What does the market dislike about existing solutions?

- The PPI itself draws real, aggregated dissatisfaction: a 4.7/10 average rating with 51% of Drugs.com reviewers reporting a negative experience (drugs.com/comments/omeprazole, accessed 2026-09-13).
- DIY tapering without guidance produces horror-story-level accounts, both Rocky Angelucci's decade of failed attempts and the reader who found the pain "unbearable" and had to resume the drug (both People's Pharmacy, accessed 2026-09-13, verbatim per search summary).
- A functional failure specific to supplements in this space: licorice-based products are not FDA-regulated for dose consistency, and can interact with other medications or dangerously lower potassium, a real caution found in this research (search-indexed supplement-safety summary, accessed 2026-09-13) that this avatar, being medically cautious, is likely to have already encountered and weighed.

### 10. Does the market believe existing solutions work?

- For the PPI itself: split and skeptical, per the Drugs.com aggregate above. This avatar does not assume "prescribed" means "safe long-term" anymore.
- For DIY tapering without support: widely believed to be risky and prone to failure, based on the repeated "tried many times without success" pattern found across HealthUnlocked threads (healthunlocked.com omeprazole-tapering cluster, accessed 2026-09-13).
- For structured, supported tapering (education plus alginate/DGL/famotidine bridge): genuinely believed to work, backed by both the UK clinical study and multiple individual patient accounts in this research. This is an important, honest finding: this avatar is not a lost cause for a supported, alginate-forward approach, the market-level evidence already supports it.

## PART III: Curiosity & Forgotten Attempts

### 11. Has anyone tried to solve this pain point in a unique or unconventional way?

- **The low-stomach-acid (hypochlorhydria) counter-theory** is the most genuinely unconventional idea found in this research: the claim that some heartburn is caused by too little acid, not too much, meaning PPIs may be treating the opposite of the real problem for a subset of sufferers (adamrinde.substack.com, untreated.substack.com, accessed 2026-09-13). It produces real curiosity and some disbelief ("wait, what if it's backwards?") precisely because it inverts the assumption this avatar has lived with for years. It has not been broadly validated by mainstream gastroenterology and should be handled as an acknowledged debate, not a claim.
- **Persimmon tea** as a folk remedy specifically tied to PPI discontinuation support is a genuinely obscure, half-forgotten-feeling attempt that still circulates in reader-comment culture (peoplespharmacy.com, accessed 2026-09-13).

### 12. Are there historical or conspiratorial stories tied to this issue?

- The clearest "suppressed information" narrative in this research is not about a blocked cure, it's about a **withheld risk disclosure**: the gap between what physicians know (87.8% aware of adverse effects) and what they tell patients (14.6% explain them) is a real, documented, citable finding, not folklore (PMC, accessed 2026-09-13). This avatar's instinct that "nobody told me the full story" is, in this specific case, factually supported.
- The "lifelong customer" framing of chronic-disease drug economics (treating is profitable, curing is not) recurs across critical commentary on the pharmaceutical industry (drhoffman.com, baddoctors.news, accessed 2026-09-13) — the latter source in particular should be flagged as an alternative-health-leaning outlet rather than a mainstream medical source, and any marketing use of this framing should stay at the level of "here's a real structural incentive worth understanding," not an accusation of deliberate harm.

## PART IV: Corruption & External Blame

### 13. Do people believe their problem is worse today than it used to be?

The belief here is less about the disease worsening and more about the **treatment relationship worsening**: a short-term drug quietly became a permanent one, at scale, across the health system, without the promised periodic review (per the NICE guidance and Medscape deprescribing coverage catalogued in the Emerging Avatar Opportunity Report, Step 2A). The explanation given is systemic drift, not personal failure or a worsening food environment (that belief belongs more to the General GERD Sufferer avatar).

### 14. Is there a clear villain or enemy in their story?

The villain is a **prescribing and healthcare-economics system that defaults to "keep refilling" rather than "keep reviewing,"** sometimes personified as "Big Pharma" in its harsher framing, more often experienced simply as "nobody ever brought it up again after the first prescription." This belief is emotionally satisfying because it locates the failure outside the patient: the problem isn't that they lack willpower to manage their health, it's that the system never gave them the chance to properly reconsider. It also makes seeking an alternative feel like taking back agency rather than admitting defeat.

### 15. Are there groups or cultures perceived as immune to the problem?

Less pronounced for this avatar specifically than for the General GERD Sufferer, since PPI dependence is a treatment-pattern problem, not a lifestyle-purity one. To the extent a contrast exists, it's temporal rather than cultural: **"the version of medicine that actually followed up,"** an implied golden age of more attentive, less rushed primary care, rather than a specific culture or country.

### 17. Emotional Hot Buttons

2. **Desire for a doctor-endorsed exit ramp**, not permission to quit recklessly.
3. **Resentment at not being told the full risk picture up front**, now compounding into distrust of "just take this" advice generally.

6. **Hope that "step down" is realistic, not just "step off or nothing,"** since a permanent full stop feels riskier than a partial, sustainable reduction.

### 2. Top Belief Chains to Leverage in Copy

**Chain 1 — The Rebound Trap:** Surface frustration: "Every time I try to stop, it gets worse." Deeper belief: stopping proves the disease was never controlled. Emotional consequence: feeling permanently stuck. Opportunity/truth: rebound is a known, temporary, explainable physiological response to stopping acid suppression, not evidence of disease progression, and a supported step-down process (education + alginate/mucosal support) measurably improves success rates.

**Chain 2 — The Withheld Risk:** Surface frustration: "Why did nobody tell me this could affect my bones/kidneys/B12?" Deeper belief: the system prioritized speed over full disclosure. Emotional consequence: resentment, self-directed research, distrust of future "just take this" advice. Opportunity/truth: a brand that explains the real, documented depletion mechanisms plainly, and offers a nutrient-replenishment angle (magnesium + B12) earns trust precisely because it fills the disclosure gap the patient already resents.

### 3. Marketing Hypotheses

1. If we speak to the **fear of rebound** using the specific clinical term "rebound" and an honest explanation of why it happens, then long-term PPI users will trust the brand's mechanism claims more than a generic "reduce your acid reflux medication" message, because this avatar has already lived through at least one failed cold-turkey attempt and specifically distrusts messaging that doesn't acknowledge that risk.

2. If we speak to the **resentment at withheld information** by naming the specific, documented nutrient depletion (magnesium, B12) their PPI causes, then this avatar will feel understood rather than marketed to, because the belief "nobody told me the full picture" is factually grounded in this population (only 14.6% of physicians who know the risks explain them), and naming it first earns permission to introduce a product second.

3. If we frame the product as **support for a structured step-down process** rather than a standalone PPI replacement, then conversion and trust will be higher than with a "get off your meds" hook, because the market-level evidence (75.1% success in a structured program) and this avatar's own successful self-reported cases (low-carb plus DGL plus famotidine) both show that staged, supported reduction, not abrupt replacement, is what this population actually believes works.

4. If we acknowledge the "lifelong customer" cynicism that exists in this audience without fully endorsing it as conspiracy, then skeptical, previously-burned buyers will find the brand more credible than either ignoring the cynicism or leaning too hard into it, because this avatar is disappointed, not radicalized, and copy that respects that distinction will read as honest rather than manipulative.
