## THE NECESSARY BELIEFS

1. **I believe that my symptoms come from a real, ongoing physical process in my body, not from something I'm doing wrong or just getting older.** Clears self-blame and "this is just aging" fatalism.
2. **I believe that most of what I've tried before failed because it only covered up the symptom, not because my reflux truly can't be helped.** Clears the fatalism built up from repeated failed attempts.
3. **I believe that something short of a prescription drug can still meaningfully help my digestion, not just calm it temporarily.** Clears the "only real medicine counts" skepticism toward supplements.
4. **I believe that a company being upfront about what's in its product, and making it easy to stop if it doesn't work, is a real sign I can trust it.** Clears distrust from subscription dark patterns and vague, hidden-dose formulas.
5. **I believe that feeling normal again is actually possible for me, not just something other people get to have.** Clears quiet despair that relief is permanently out of reach.
6. **I believe that trying this specific product now is a safe, low-cost next step, not one more disappointment I can't afford to risk.** Clears the final purchase hesitation and guarantee skepticism.

## THE BELIEF SEQUENCE

He already believes, without needing to be told, that something about modern food and modern life changed and that's a real part of why his gut is like this now. That belief is useful because it already puts the cause outside of him, not on him. The first place to meet him is right there: not "it's the food," specifically, but the plainer version underneath it, that his body is responding to something real and ongoing, not failing on its own or simply aging. Once he accepts that his symptoms are a physical process with a cause, rather than a personal shortcoming, the next question becomes obvious: if there's a real cause, why hasn't anything fixed it yet?

That's where his history of failed attempts has to be reframed, carefully, because he is not wrong that those things didn't work, he just has the wrong explanation for why. He currently believes it means nothing can help. He needs to move to believing those attempts failed because they only quieted the symptom without touching what was actually going on underneath, which is a much smaller, easier claim to accept than "you just hadn't found the right product yet." Once he accepts that distinction, between covering something up and actually supporting it, he's ready for a bigger question: does anything short of his prescription even count as real help? Here his own guardedness works in his favor once it's respected rather than argued with. He already knows the category is full of overreach, so the belief he needs is a modest one: not "this will fix you," but "something outside a prescription can genuinely help," which is a claim he's already seen at least a few credible people (a skeptical dietitian, a cautious reviewer) come around to after actually testing something.

That opens the door to trust, but trust in a category, not yet trust in a company. He has been burned specifically by subscription tricks and vague ingredient lists, so before he'll consider anyone's product, he needs to believe that honesty about ingredients and an easy way out are the real signal of a trustworthy brand, not marketing language. Only once that belief is in place does his own quiet hope become usable: he already wants to feel normal again, he just doesn't yet believe it's realistic for him specifically. Naming that gap and closing it, showing him that this isn't only for other people, is what turns a private longing into an active reason to act. And the very last belief he needs is not really new information at all, it's permission: that trying this now is a small, safe step, not a repeat of every other disappointment, which is exactly why an honest, verifiable guarantee matters so much to him at the very end of the argument.

## NOTES

- **Live wires, never contradict:** His fatigue from repeated failed attempts is real and earned, don't imply he simply hasn't tried hard enough. His distrust of subscription and guarantee language is well-founded (documented complaint patterns against real competitors); never ask him to just "trust us," always show him the honesty rather than claim it.
- **Thin evidence:** Necessary Belief 3 ("something short of a prescription can help") rests mostly on one dietitian's account and general category skepticism-then-belief patterns, not a large volume of independently verified testimonials. Treat it as directionally right, not proven at scale.
- **Gaps to fill:** The research doesn't show this avatar's current belief about supplement guarantees specifically before he's been burned once; it shows the burn (subscription complaints) but not his baseline expectation walking in. Worth a targeted question in future research: "what do you expect a money-back guarantee to actually mean before you've been disappointed by one?"
